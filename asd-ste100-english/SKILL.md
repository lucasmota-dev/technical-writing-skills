# ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English — Issue 9

## Purpose

Help readers understand technical information correctly on the first read. Use clear, controlled English without changing the technical meaning, facts, limits, conditions, safety information, or intended result.

Use this skill for technical procedures, maintenance and operating instructions, descriptions, troubleshooting, user manuals, help content, warnings, requirements, and related technical documentation.

## Authority and limitations

Use **ASD-STE100 Issue 9, dated 15 January 2025**, as the reference for this skill.

- Official site: https://www.asd-ste100.org/
- Official standard and dictionary access: https://www.asd-ste100.org/STE_downloads.html

ASD-STE100 has two parts: **Part 1, Writing rules**, and **Part 2, Dictionary**. Part 1 contains nine sections and 53 rules. Part 2 defines approved vocabulary, meanings, parts of speech, and permitted verb and adjective forms, and provides word-selection guidance.

This file is a practical summary, not a reproduction of the standard or its dictionary. The official ASD document takes precedence if this skill conflicts with it. **Do not claim full STE compliance unless the applicable Issue 9 rules and dictionary entries have been checked.** If the complete dictionary is not available, describe the result as *STE-inspired* or *simplified technical English*, not as verified STE.

STE does not replace applicable publication specifications, safety requirements, company style guides, or domain requirements. The standard does not define document formatting, numbering, abbreviation policy, or a universal method for expressing units of measurement. Follow the applicable project or industry rules for those topics.

## 1. Select and control words

For strict STE writing, check each relevant word against Part 2 of the official dictionary.

- Use dictionary words only with an approved meaning, part of speech, and form.
- Do not assume that a word is approved for every grammatical use or every meaning found in general English.
- When a word is not approved, consult its dictionary entry and use a permitted alternative or restructure the sentence. Do not make a word-for-word substitution if it changes the meaning.
- Use a technical noun only when it names a defined concept in the applicable subject field and is permitted by the technical-noun rules. Issue 9 gives 22 categories to help identify these nouns. Use the company or industry glossary where available.
- Use a technical verb only when it names an applicable technical action or process and is permitted by the technical-verb rules.
- Do not use technical nouns as verbs or technical verbs as nouns. Check the term's defined role and the applicable rule.
- Do not use regional expressions, slang, or informal jargon as technical nouns.
- Do not use different technical nouns for the same item. Select one approved term and use it consistently.
- When you must select a technical noun, prefer a short term that readers can understand.
- Use American English spelling unless an applicable official directive requires another form.

In the Part 2 word list, an entry in uppercase is approved and an entry in lowercase is not approved. Still check the complete entry: capitalization alone does not tell you which meaning, part of speech, or form is allowed. Use the dictionary's word-selection flowchart, recurring-error list, and approved-verb list as aids, not as replacements for checking the specific entry.

## 2. Control multi-word nouns

- Keep ordinary multi-word noun groups to **three words or fewer** when this can be done without changing the technical meaning.
- If an official technical noun is longer, do not rename or shorten it in a way that changes its identity. Write it in full when first needed, then introduce a clear shorter form or an approved abbreviation when appropriate.
- Use a hyphen only when it correctly shows that words belong together. Do not add hyphens merely to bypass the three-word rule, and do not alter an official technical term's spelling.
- Prefer a clear construction with prepositions or a relative clause when it makes the relationship between parts easier to understand.

## 3. Use approved verb forms

Use the forms specified for each approved verb in the dictionary. Issue 9 permits these general forms and tenses when the particular verb supports them:

- infinitive/base form;
- imperative form for commands;
- simple present;
- simple past;
- simple future;
- past participle as an adjective, when that form is approved.

Do not introduce unapproved progressive or perfect constructions, or complex auxiliary constructions, to make the wording sound more natural. Restructure the sentence with an approved verb form. Do not assume that every regular or irregular form is permitted; check the dictionary.

Use the **active voice**. In descriptive writing, use the passive voice only when the agent is unknown. Name the person, system, or component that performs the action when it is known and relevant.

Do not use a verb's **-ing** form as an ordinary verb form or adjective unless the applicable rule permits it. Issue 9 limits this form to technical nouns and modifiers within technical nouns, with specific approved dictionary entries as exceptions. Check the rule and dictionary before using it.

Describe an action with an approved verb rather than an abstract noun or another part of speech when the rules require a verb.

## 4. Write clear sentences

- Write short, direct sentences that express one main idea.
- Do not omit words or use contractions just to make a sentence shorter.
- Avoid nested clauses, ambiguous references, long modifier groups, and unnecessary parenthetical information.
- Make the subject, action, object, condition, and result clear where they matter.
- Use connecting words or phrases when they show how sentences relate.
- Use a vertical list when it makes complex information easier to follow.
- Use articles and demonstrative adjectives when applicable to make the reference clear.
- Do not keep a sentence structure that becomes unclear after a dictionary word is replaced. Change the construction when needed.
- Do not create phrasal verbs by combining words in a way that is not approved.
- Use punctuation correctly. **Do not use semicolons in STE**; split the text into separate sentences instead.

## 5. Write procedures

Apply the procedural-writing rules in Section 5 of Part 1.

- Keep each procedural sentence to **20 words maximum**. Apply the Issue 9 word-count rules in Section 8; do not estimate by visual length.
- Give one instruction in each sentence, unless two or more actions are part of the same operation and belong together.
- Start instructions with an approved verb in the imperative form.
- Put a condition first when the reader must know it before acting.
- Put steps in the order the reader must perform them.
- Identify the exact component, control, field, file, menu, or tool by its approved name.
- State prerequisites before the step that depends on them.
- Add an expected result only when it is known and useful for confirming completion.
- Place the applicable warning or caution before the action that creates the risk.
- Use a note to provide information, not to hide an extra instruction. Put required actions in the procedure itself.
- Do not state that a step succeeded unless the source or system behavior confirms it.

Use a numbered sequence when order is required. Use a vertical list for choices, conditions, or items that are easier to scan than to read in a paragraph.

## 6. Write descriptions

Apply the descriptive-writing rules in Section 6 of Part 1.

- Present information gradually, in a logical order.
- Use key words and phrases to make the structure visible.
- Keep each descriptive sentence to **25 words maximum**. Apply the official word-count rules.
- Group related information in the same paragraph.
- Keep one topic in each paragraph.
- Use no more than **six sentences per paragraph**.
- Name components, relationships, functions, conditions, and results precisely.
- Do not mix a procedure into a descriptive paragraph when readers need step-by-step instructions.
- Use the passive voice only when the agent is unknown.

## 7. Write safety instructions carefully

Use the safety terminology and risk levels required by the applicable industry, product, and regulatory rules. Do not invent a risk level or replace an established safety label with a different one.

For the STE examples described in Issue 9:

- A **WARNING** identifies a risk of injury or death.
- A **CAUTION** identifies a risk of damage to objects or equipment.
- In the risk scheme described by Issue 9, use a **WARNING** when both types of risk are present. Also check the safety specification that applies to the actual industry and product. Do not downgrade the risk.

A safety instruction must:

1. identify the applicable risk level with the required word or symbol;
2. start with a clear, accurate command or a necessary condition;
3. explain the hazard, risk, or possible result when that information is known.

Preserve approved warnings and safety requirements exactly where required. Simplify the wording only when permitted and without weakening, omitting, or changing the hazard, consequence, or required action. If the risk is unclear, flag it for expert review instead of guessing.

## 8. Check punctuation and word count

Apply Section 8 of Part 1 when strict STE compliance is required.

- Do not use semicolons.
- Use hyphens only to connect words that are directly related; preserve established technical terms.
- Follow the rule for parentheses and the prescribed word-count treatment.
- Count numbers, numbers with units, abbreviations, alphanumeric identifiers, quoted text, headings, labels, and specified proper nouns according to Rule 8.6.
- Count a hyphenated word as one word, as specified by Rule 8.7.
- For lists, apply the rules that determine where a sentence ends and how its words are counted.

Do not make a compliance claim based on an approximate count. When exact counts matter, count according to Issue 9 rather than an ordinary word processor's default behavior.

## 9. Maintain consistent writing practices

Use Section 9 and its general recommendations as a final review:

- Use a different sentence construction when a direct word replacement is not sufficient.
- Use every approved word with the correct meaning and grammatical function.
- Avoid unapproved phrasal verbs.
- Keep terminology and style consistent across the document set.
- Check the guidance on *that*, *with*, pronouns, *this*, false friends, Latin abbreviations, inclusive language, and possessive forms when relevant.
- Prefer references that identify the exact item. Replace unclear pronouns when the reader could confuse their meaning.
- Use inclusive, accurate wording without changing technical facts or established role names.

## 10. Workflow

1. **Understand the task.** Identify the audience, document type, purpose, and actions or facts that must remain unchanged.
2. **Extract the source facts.** Record approved product terms, identifiers, values, units, conditions, limits, prerequisites, exceptions, and safety content.
3. **Select terminology.** Check dictionary entries and the applicable company or industry glossary.
4. **Draft.** Choose procedural or descriptive structure as appropriate. Use direct sentences and consistent terms.
5. **Check the rules.** Review vocabulary, part of speech, approved meaning and verb forms, noun-group length, sentence construction, punctuation, and applicable word limits.
6. **Check safety.** Verify labels, hazards, consequences, conditions, and required actions against authoritative source material.
7. **Compare with the source.** Confirm that no requirement, cause, behavior, promise, limit, or result has been added, removed, or changed.
8. **Report compliance honestly.** If the full dictionary or a rule cannot be checked, identify the limitation and do not claim verified STE compliance.

## Final checklist

- Is every general word checked against the Issue 9 dictionary when strict compliance is requested?
- Is each approved word used with the correct meaning, part of speech, and permitted form?
- Are technical nouns and technical verbs valid for the subject field and consistent with the glossary?
- Are multi-word nouns controlled without changing official technical terms?
- Are verb forms, voice, sentence construction, and phrasal verbs consistent with the rules?
- Are procedure sentences at most 20 words and descriptive sentences at most 25 words, counted by Issue 9?
- Does each descriptive paragraph contain one topic and no more than six sentences?
- Are procedural steps imperative, ordered, and clear about conditions?
- Are safety instructions accurate and at the correct risk level?
- Are punctuation and word-count rules satisfied?
- Are spelling, terminology, and style consistent?
- Does the text preserve all facts, constraints, exceptions, and technical meaning?
- Is any claim of STE compliance supported by an actual check against the official standard and dictionary?

## Response behavior

- When asked to write, provide the requested text directly.
- When asked to simplify or rewrite, preserve the original intent and every material technical fact.
- When asked to review, identify meaningful issues and propose corrections.
- Do not invent technical behavior, causes, limits, warnings, or results.
- If a missing fact affects correctness or safety, ask one focused question or mark it as unresolved.
- Do not add lengthy explanations or multiple versions unless requested.
